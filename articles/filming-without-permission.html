Can Someone Legally Film You Without Your Permission?

Someone points their phone at you in the street and starts recording. You tell them to stop. Do they legally have to?

Law Simplified • 3 min read

Imagine you're walking through town.

You notice someone filming.

You appear clearly in the video, but nobody asked for your permission.

It might feel like they must be breaking the law.

But in England and Wales, there is no general rule requiring an ordinary member of the public to get your consent simply because they film you in a public place.

Members of the public generally do not need a permit simply to photograph or film in public.

That does not, however, mean that anything recorded in public can automatically be obtained or used however someone wants.

01

What's the problem?

There is a difference between being visible in public and having no privacy at all.

Imagine accidentally appearing in the background of someone's video.

Now compare that with somebody deliberately following a person, repeatedly recording them and later publishing footage revealing aspects of their private life.

Both situations can involve filming in public.

But legally, they are not necessarily the same.

IN SIMPLE TERMS

What is a reasonable expectation of privacy?

This asks whether, considering all the circumstances, a person could reasonably expect the particular information or activity to remain private.

Being somewhere public does not automatically destroy every possible expectation of privacy.

Things such as where someone is, what they are doing, their age, how the footage was obtained and what happens to it afterwards can all matter.

02

What does the law say?

For ordinary photography or filming in a public place, permission from every person who appears in the image is not generally required.

But that is only the starting point.

Privacy

Where photographs, footage or information involve someone's private life, the law of misuse of private information may become relevant.

Courts broadly ask two important questions.

First: did the person have a reasonable expectation of privacy?

If they did, the court must then consider the competing rights involved, particularly the right to respect for private and family life under Article 8 and freedom of expression under Article 10.

Neither right automatically wins simply because it appears in the case.

Data protection

Data-protection law can also matter where organisations use video surveillance to process information about identifiable people.

Organisations subject to the UK GDPR and Data Protection Act 2018 must have an appropriate lawful basis and comply with principles including fairness, transparency and data minimisation.

This is different from assuming that every individual using a phone for purely personal purposes automatically has exactly the same obligations.

Harassment

The way somebody films can also matter.

Repeatedly following and filming someone against their wishes could potentially form part of harassment or stalking depending on the circumstances.

Under the Protection from Harassment Act 1997, harassment involves a course of conduct, meaning conduct occurring on at least two occasions.

“You were in public” can be the beginning of the legal question — not always the end of it.

03

Why does this matter?

Almost everyone now carries a camera.

A phone can record, upload and potentially show footage to thousands or millions of people within minutes.

That makes the boundary between public life and private life harder to draw.

A rule requiring permission every time another person appeared in a photograph or video would be extremely difficult to operate.

But saying “you were outside, so you have zero privacy” goes too far in the opposite direction.

Modern privacy law therefore has to look closely at context.

THINKING ABOUT THE LAW

Where should the line be drawn?

I think filming in public should generally be allowed.

Otherwise ordinary photography, journalism and even casual phone videos would become extremely difficult.

But context matters.

There is a clear difference between accidentally recording someone in the background and deliberately targeting a person, repeatedly following them or publishing details of their private life.

The difficult legal question is therefore no longer simply:

“Was this filmed in public?”

It can also be:

“What was being recorded, how was it recorded, and what was done with it afterwards?”

THE TAKEAWAY

So, what should you remember?

You do not automatically have a legal right to stop another member of the public filming you simply because you did not give permission.

But being in public does not mean losing every possible privacy protection. The circumstances, the way footage is obtained and what happens to it afterwards can change the legal position.
