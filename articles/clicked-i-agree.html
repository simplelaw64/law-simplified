You Clicked “I Agree” — But Did You Actually Agree?

We accept terms and conditions all the time without reading them. So how can they still legally bind us — and is there anything stopping businesses from hiding nasty surprises in the small print?

Law Simplified • 3 min read

You download an app. Create an account. Tick “I agree to the Terms and Conditions.” Done.

Except behind that tiny box might be pages explaining payments, cancellations, renewals and other rules you never actually read.

So have you really agreed to them?

In many situations, yes. Simply saying “I didn't read it” does not automatically get you out of a contract.

That might sound unfair, but imagine the alternative. If anyone could escape a contract by claiming they had not read it, agreements would become almost impossible to rely on.

The more interesting question is therefore not whether you read every word.

It is: how far can a business go when deciding what to put in those unread terms?

IN SIMPLE TERMS

What is a contract term?

A contract term is simply part of the agreement between the people or businesses making the contract.

It might cover the price, what you are buying, when you can cancel or what happens if something goes wrong.

But writing something in the small print does not automatically mean a business can enforce it.

02

What does the law say?

Consumer law puts limits on what businesses can do with their terms.

Under the Consumer Rights Act 2015, terms in consumer contracts must be fair. An unfair term is not binding on the consumer.

Written terms must also be transparent: they should be written in plain, intelligible language and be legible.

For certain important terms dealing with the main subject matter or price of a contract, being clear is especially important. The law limits when those terms can be protected from a fairness assessment unless they are both transparent and prominent.

The Competition and Markets Authority, or CMA, also tells businesses to think carefully about how significant terms are presented rather than relying on lengthy small print that customers are unlikely to notice.

Think about a subscription.

£4.99 for the first month might appear in huge letters.

But suppose the fact that it later becomes £24.99 is difficult to find.

The problem is no longer just whether the information technically existed somewhere. The question becomes whether the customer was given a fair opportunity to understand what they were signing up for.

And making a term obvious does not automatically make it lawful.

A shop could display “NO REFUNDS” in enormous letters, but that cannot simply remove the legal remedies consumers may have when goods are faulty.

The real issue is not whether every word was read, but whether businesses can rely on unfair surprises hidden inside the agreement.

03

So who should be responsible?

There is a fair argument on both sides.

Consumers cannot expect to ignore every agreement they enter and then reject any term they dislike.

But businesses also have much more control. They write the terms. They decide what gets highlighted and what gets buried.

That makes me think there should be a difference between an ordinary condition and something that could seriously affect someone's decision.

A major price increase, automatic renewal or difficult cancellation condition matters far more to most customers than a minor technical clause.

However, highlighting absolutely everything would not solve the problem either. If every website showed fifteen warnings before checkout, people would probably start ignoring those too.

The challenge is deciding which information matters enough that a consumer should genuinely see it before clicking “I agree.”

THE TAKEAWAY

So, what should you remember?

Clicking “I agree” can matter even when you haven't read every word. But businesses cannot assume that hiding something inside pages of terms makes it untouchable. Consumer law tries to balance two things: contracts need to be reliable, but agreement should not become an excuse for unfair surprises.
