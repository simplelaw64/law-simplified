EMPLOYMENT LAW

Your Contract Says You're Self-Employed — Does That Make It True?

Your contract might call you “self-employed”, but that does not always decide your legal rights. Employment law can look at what your job actually looks like in real life.

Law Simplified • 3 min read

Imagine your contract says “self-employed”.

You work for a company through an app.

The company sets the price customers pay, controls important rules and can restrict your access to work.

But your contract says:

“You are self-employed.”

Does that settle it?

Not necessarily.

In employment law, what happens in reality can matter more than the label written on the contract.

01

Why does your employment status matter?

In Great Britain, there are three main employment-status categories used when deciding employment rights.

An employee generally receives the widest range of statutory employment rights, although some rights depend on other conditions such as length of service.

A worker receives important protections including the National Minimum Wage and paid holiday, but generally has fewer rights than an employee.

Someone who is genuinely self-employed normally runs their own business and has far fewer employment-law protections.

So employment status can make a major difference to someone's legal rights.

IN SIMPLE TERMS

What is employment status?

Employment status describes the legal relationship between someone doing work and the person or business they work for.

It helps determine which statutory employment rights apply.

Importantly, someone's status for employment rights can differ from their status for tax purposes.

02

What does the law look at?

There is no single question that decides every employment-status case.

Courts and employment tribunals examine the reality of the working relationship.

Several factors may matter.

How much control does the business have over the way the work is performed?

Does the individual have to perform the work personally, or can they genuinely send someone else instead?

How much freedom does the person have over when, where and how they work?

Are they genuinely operating a business on their own account, taking commercial risk and dealing with clients?

The contract remains relevant evidence.

But it is not always the final answer where its wording does not match what actually happens.

Calling someone “self-employed” does not automatically make the legal relationship self-employment.

03

Why does this matter?

The rule prevents employment rights from being determined entirely by whoever writes the contract.

Imagine someone works under significant company control but signs paperwork calling them an “independent contractor”.

If that label automatically decided everything, a business could potentially avoid protections such as holiday pay or minimum-wage rights simply by changing the wording of its documents.

But there is another side.

Some people genuinely want to work for themselves because self-employment can provide much greater freedom over clients, prices, working patterns and business decisions.

The law therefore has to distinguish genuine independence from arrangements that look independent mainly on paper.

THINKING ABOUT THE LAW

Where does flexibility end and dependence begin?

I think looking at reality rather than simply accepting the contractual label is fair.

Businesses should be able to offer genuinely flexible ways of working.

But writing “self-employed” should not automatically remove legal protections if the person actually works under significant control and dependence.

The difficult part is drawing the line.

Someone can have flexibility over when they work while still having very little control over the price, terms or way the work is carried out.

That means flexibility alone cannot tell the whole story.

THE TAKEAWAY

So, what should you remember?

Your employment status is not decided by a label alone. A contract may describe someone as self-employed, but a court or tribunal can examine how the relationship actually operates before deciding which employment protections apply.
