CORPORATE LAW

If Your Company Goes Bust, Do You Have to Pay Its Debts?

You can own a company, control it and make money from it — but its debts are not automatically yours. Why?

Law Simplified • 3 min read

Imagine your company owes £50,000.

You start a limited company. It borrows money, signs contracts and buys equipment.

Then the business fails.

Can the people it owes money to simply come after your personal savings?

Usually, not simply because you own the company.

That is because the law treats a registered company as legally separate from its shareholders.

01

What's the problem?

When a company is incorporated, it becomes its own legal body.

Lawyers call this separate legal personality.

The company can own property, enter contracts, borrow money and owe debts in its own name.

So even if one shareholder owns every share, the shareholder and the company are not legally the same person.

IN SIMPLE TERMS

What is limited liability?

Separate legal personality and limited liability are connected, but they are not exactly the same idea.

Separate legal personality means the company exists as a legal person separate from its shareholders.

Limited liability means that, in a company limited by shares, a shareholder's liability is normally limited to any amount still unpaid on their shares.

If the company fails, shareholders may lose what they invested, but they do not normally become personally responsible for all of the company's debts simply because they own shares.

02

What does the law say?

Under the Companies Act 2006, a company can be registered as limited or unlimited.

Section 16 provides that once the registration requirements are completed, the subscribers and future members become a body corporate.

In other words, the company exists as its own legal entity.

For a company limited by shares, the liability of its members is limited in the way set out by company law.

But none of this means:

“I own a limited company, so I can never be personally responsible for anything.”

A director can still face consequences for their own wrongdoing. A person who personally guarantees company borrowing may also have to honour that guarantee.

And English law allows the company's separate personality to be disregarded only in very limited circumstances.

03

Why does this matter?

Imagine you supply a company with £10,000 of goods and it collapses before paying you.

The shareholder might lose their investment.

But you may also lose some or all of your £10,000 if the company does not have enough assets to pay its creditors.

That is the difficult side of the system.

Limited liability encourages investment and entrepreneurship because business failure does not automatically expose every shareholder's personal assets.

But that protection can also mean that creditors carry some of the risk when a company fails.

THINKING ABOUT THE LAW

Is that protection fair?

I think the basic rule makes sense.

People would be far less willing to create companies or invest in them if one failed business could automatically cost them everything they personally owned.

But there also need to be limits.

The law should protect genuine business risk, not give people an easy way to misuse a company to escape responsibilities they already have.

That tension explains why modern company law strongly protects separate legal personality while still recognising narrow exceptions and separate rules dealing with misconduct.

THE TAKEAWAY

So, what should you remember?

A registered company is legally separate from the people who own it. If it is a company limited by shares, shareholders are not normally personally responsible for all of its debts simply because they own the company. That separation is one of the foundations of modern corporate law.
