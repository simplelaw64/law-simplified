CRIMINAL LAW

Can You Be Guilty of Murder Without Intending to Kill?

Most people assume murder means deliberately deciding to kill someone. English law is more complicated: intending really serious harm can also be enough.

Law Simplified • 3 min read

Imagine someone intends to cause another person really serious injury, but does not intend for them to die. The victim dies anyway.

It might seem obvious that this cannot be murder because there was no intention to kill.

Under the law of England and Wales, however, it can be.

That raises a difficult question: should someone who intended serious injury be treated as a murderer when death was never their aim?

01

What's the problem?

For murder, the prosecution normally has to prove both an unlawful killing and the required state of mind.

That state of mind does not have to be: “I intended to kill.”

An intention to cause grievous bodily harm — really serious bodily injury — can also be enough for murder.

That creates a gap between what many people think the word murder means and what the law actually requires.

IN SIMPLE TERMS

What is mens rea?

Mens rea means the mental element required for a criminal offence.

It asks what the defendant intended, knew or sometimes foresaw when acting.

For murder, the relevant intention can be either an intention to kill or an intention to cause really serious bodily harm.

02

What does the law say?

Murder is mainly a common-law offence. Its definition has developed through court decisions rather than being contained in one modern Act of Parliament.

The important point is that a person does not necessarily have to want death to happen.

If they unlawfully kill someone while intending really serious bodily harm, that intention can satisfy the mental element required for murder.

Interestingly, attempted murder is narrower. For attempted murder, an intention to kill is required. An intention only to cause serious injury is not enough.

Murder also carries a mandatory life sentence. That does not automatically mean someone will spend the rest of their life in prison. The court determines the minimum period that must normally be served before release can be considered, and a person released from a life sentence remains subject to licence for life.

Intending really serious injury is extremely serious — but is it the same level of intention as intending death?

Two ways of looking at it

There is a strong argument for the current rule.

If someone deliberately intends extremely serious harm and the victim dies, the law may reasonably treat that person as highly responsible for the death.

Requiring proof that they specifically wanted death could push some very serious killings outside the offence of murder.

But there is also a problem.

Intending serious injury is not the same mental state as intending death.

If murder represents the most serious form of unlawful killing, should the law recognise a difference between those two intentions?

03

Why does this matter?

This is not just an academic argument.

A murder conviction carries enormous legal consequences, including the mandatory life sentence.

The difference between intending death and intending serious injury can therefore affect both the label attached to an offence and the sentencing framework that follows.

And the debate is current.

In June 2026, the Law Commission opened a consultation on reforming homicide offences.

One provisional proposal would divide murder into first-degree murder, where there was an intention to kill, and second-degree murder, where there was an intention to cause serious injury.

Under the proposal, first-degree murder would carry the mandatory life sentence. Second-degree murder would have a maximum sentence of life imprisonment, allowing greater sentencing discretion.

These are proposals, not the current law.

THINKING ABOUT THE LAW

Does the current rule go too far?

I understand why an intention to cause serious injury can lead to murder liability. Someone should not escape responsibility simply because death was not their exact aim.

But I think there is a genuine difference between wanting someone dead and wanting them seriously injured.

Treating both automatically as the same offence risks hiding that difference.

The Law Commission's proposed two-level system is interesting because it would still treat both as extremely serious forms of homicide while recognising that their levels of intention are not identical.

THE TAKEAWAY

So, what should you remember?

Yes — in England and Wales, someone can be guilty of murder without intending to kill. An intention to cause really serious bodily harm can also be enough. Whether those two forms of intention should continue to receive the same label is now part of a live debate about reforming homicide law.
