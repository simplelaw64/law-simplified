If a Business Breaks a Contract, How Much Money Should You Get?

If a business breaks a deal and you lose money, can you claim every penny you lost? Not necessarily. Contract law puts limits on how much compensation can be recovered.

Law Simplified • 3 min read

Imagine a supplier lets your business down.

You own a café and order a new coffee machine for Monday. The supplier promises to deliver it but does not.

Without the machine, you lose sales.

Can you demand compensation?

Probably for some losses — but not automatically every loss that follows from the breach.

That is where contract damages come in.

01

What's the problem?

When a contract is broken, the innocent party can suffer several different losses.

They might lose profits, have to pay more for a replacement supplier or face other unexpected costs.

But how far should responsibility go?

Imagine the delayed coffee machine somehow causes the café to lose a £100,000 business opportunity that the supplier knew nothing about.

Should the supplier automatically have to pay £100,000?

English contract law says there must be limits.

IN SIMPLE TERMS

What are damages?

Damages are money awarded to compensate someone for loss caused by a legal wrong.

In contract law, the basic aim is normally to put the innocent party, so far as money can do it, in the position they would have been in if the contract had been properly performed.

They are generally about compensation, not punishment.

02

What does the law say?

Several limits matter when a court works out contractual damages.

1. What did you actually lose?

The claimant needs to show that the breach actually caused loss.

A court is not simply giving someone money because the other business behaved badly.

2. Was the loss too remote?

Not every consequence of a breach can be recovered.

The classic rule comes from Hadley v Baxendale.

Broadly, contractual damages may cover losses that arise naturally in the ordinary course from the breach, or losses resulting from special circumstances that were sufficiently known to the parties when they made the contract.

If one party faces a highly unusual risk and the other party knows nothing about it, making them responsible for that unexpected loss can be much harder.

3. Could you have reduced the loss?

The innocent party also has to act reasonably after the breach.

This is known as mitigation.

If sensible steps could have reduced an avoidable loss but the claimant unreasonably failed to take them, they may not recover that avoidable amount.

Breaking a contract can create liability — but it does not make one business responsible for every consequence, however unexpected.

03

Why does this matter?

Businesses rely on contracts constantly.

If every breach made a business responsible for every unexpected consequence, entering contracts could become extremely risky.

But if damages were too limited, contracts would provide much less protection when promises were broken.

The law therefore tries to balance compensating the innocent party with keeping liability within reasonable limits.

THINKING ABOUT THE LAW

Where should responsibility stop?

I think the basic balance makes sense.

If a business breaks its promise, it should compensate the other side for losses that the breach genuinely and reasonably caused.

But it seems unfair to make someone responsible for an enormous and unusual loss they could not realistically have understood when agreeing to the contract.

The rule also gives businesses a reason to communicate unusual risks before signing.

If one side knows that even a small delay could cause an extraordinary loss, saying that beforehand can make a major difference.

THE TAKEAWAY

So, what should you remember?

Breaking a contract does not mean writing the other side a blank cheque. Contract damages aim to compensate genuine loss, but recovery is limited by rules including causation, remoteness and mitigation.
