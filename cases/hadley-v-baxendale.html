CASE STUDY • COMMERCIAL LAW • CONTRACT LAW

Hadley v Baxendale

(1854) 9 Ex 341; 156 ER 145; [1854] EWHC Exch J70

A delivery arrived late and a mill lost money while it waited. How far should contractual liability extend?

COURT

Court of Exchequer

YEAR

1854

AREA OF LAW

Commercial / Contract

READ TIME

3 minutes

THE CASE IN ONE MINUTE

What happened?

A mill's crankshaft broke. Baxendale's carrier business was hired to transport it so a replacement could be made.

The delivery was delayed and the mill owners claimed lost profits.

The court held that those profits were too remote on the facts because the relevant special circumstances had not been sufficiently communicated.

01

The Facts

The broken shaft had to be sent to engineers so it could be used as a model for a replacement.

The carrier delayed delivery. The mill owners said the delay kept the mill closed and caused lost profits.

THE BIG QUESTION

Should someone who breaks a contract have to pay for every financial loss that follows?

02

What did each side argue?

HADLEY

The delay caused the loss

The mill owners said the delayed delivery caused the continued shutdown and lost profits.

BAXENDALE

The profits were too remote

The carrier argued that it had not been given enough information to understand the unusual financial consequences of delay.

YOUR TURN

What would you decide?

Should Baxendale have had to compensate the mill owners for the claimed lost profits?

YOUR DECISION

WHY A LIMIT IS NEEDED

One breach can cause a long chain of consequences.

Without a boundary, a single broken promise could create liability for increasingly unexpected losses.

03

What did the court decide?

Baxendale succeeded on the issue of the claimed lost profits.

The court distinguished losses arising naturally from the breach from losses dependent on special circumstances known to both parties.

IN SIMPLE TERMS

If an unusual risk matters, tell the other side.

That can make a major difference to whether the loss is recoverable.

THE LEGAL PRINCIPLE

Remoteness of contractual damages

A party is not automatically responsible for every consequence of breach.

04

Why does this case matter?

Hadley remains a starting point for contractual remoteness and encourages businesses to communicate unusual risks.

THINKING ABOUT THE CASE

Was the principle fair?

I think it is fair to compensate genuine loss while limiting liability for consequences the other side could not reasonably understand.

THE TAKEAWAY

If you remember one thing...

Breaking a contract does not make someone responsible for every loss that follows.
