CASE STUDY • CORPORATE LAW

Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd

[1896] UKHL 1; [1897] AC 22

Aron Salomon owned almost an entire company himself. When it failed, creditors argued he should personally pay its debts.

COURT

House of Lords

YEAR

1896

AREA OF LAW

Corporate Law

READ TIME

3 minutes

THE CASE IN ONE MINUTE

What happened?

Salomon transferred his boot-making business to A Salomon & Co Ltd and ultimately held 20,001 of its 20,007 shares.

The company later failed. The House of Lords held that it was nevertheless legally separate from Salomon.

01

The Facts

Salomon incorporated a company and transferred his business to it. Almost all shares were held by him, with six family members each holding one share.

When the company became insolvent, the liquidator argued that Salomon and the company were effectively the same business.

THE BIG QUESTION

Can someone own and control almost an entire company but still be legally separate from it?

02

What did each side argue?

LIQUIDATOR

The company was really Salomon

Because Salomon controlled almost everything, the liquidator argued he should bear responsibility for the company's debts.

SALOMON

The company was legally incorporated

Salomon argued that once the legal requirements were followed, the company became a separate legal person.

YOUR TURN

What would you decide?

Should Salomon personally have been responsible for the company's debts?

03

What did the court decide?

Salomon won.

The House of Lords held that the company was legally separate from him. Its debts were not automatically his personal debts.

IN SIMPLE TERMS

Think of the company as its own legal person.

You may own it and control it, but you are still not legally the same person as the company.

THE LEGAL PRINCIPLE

Separate legal personality

A properly incorporated company is legally separate from its shareholders.

04

Why does this case matter?

This principle sits at the centre of modern company law and explains why company debts are normally pursued against the company itself.

THINKING ABOUT THE CASE

Was the decision convincing?

I think the decision makes legal sense because Salomon followed the incorporation rules, although the case also shows how creditors can sometimes be left unpaid.

THE TAKEAWAY

If you remember one thing...

A properly incorporated company is legally separate from the people who own it.
