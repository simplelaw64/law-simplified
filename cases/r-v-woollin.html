CASE STUDY • CRIMINAL LAW

R v Woollin

[1998] UKHL 28; [1999] 1 AC 82

What if someone does not want a result to happen but understands that it is virtually certain because of what they are doing? Woollin explores where taking a serious risk ends and intention can begin.

COURT

House of Lords

YEAR

1998

AREA OF LAW

Criminal Law

READ TIME

3 minutes

THE CASE IN ONE MINUTE

What happened?

Woollin lost his temper and threw his three-month-old son. The child died from the resulting injuries.

Woollin denied intending to kill or cause really serious harm.

At trial, the judge told the jury that they could find intention if Woollin had appreciated a substantial risk of serious injury.

He was convicted of murder.

The House of Lords decided that this test was too broad. The murder conviction was quashed and manslaughter substituted.

01

The Facts

The prosecution did not argue that Woollin actually wanted his son to die or suffer really serious injury.

The difficult issue was what Woollin foresaw when he acted.

That mattered because murder required the prosecution to prove an intention either to kill or to cause really serious harm.

At trial, the judge told the jury they could find intention if Woollin appreciated that there was a substantial risk of serious injury.

Woollin was convicted of murder and appealed.

THE BIG QUESTION

When can a result someone did not actually want still be treated as something they intended?

The case forced the House of Lords to consider the boundary between simply recognising a risk and having the intention required for murder.

02

What did each side argue?

WOOLLIN

Risk is not intention

Woollin argued that appreciating a risk of serious harm was not enough to prove intention.

A person can recognise that something dangerous might happen without actually intending that result.

PROSECUTION

The consequences still mattered

The prosecution argued that the jury was entitled to consider Woollin's actions and what he must have understood about their likely consequences.

The wider issue was how high that level of foresight had to be before intention could be found.

YOUR TURN

What would you decide?

Was the evidence of intention strong enough for Woollin's murder conviction to stand?

BEFORE WOOLLIN

What did the law already say?

An earlier case, R v Nedrick, had developed a much higher threshold.

It suggested that a jury should not find the necessary intention unless death or serious harm was a virtual certainty from the defendant's actions and the defendant appreciated that fact.

The question in Woollin was whether that demanding approach should remain.

03

What did the court decide?

Woollin's appeal succeeded.

The House of Lords decided that the trial judge had set the threshold too low.

A substantial risk is not the same as a virtual certainty.

Where direct intention is not proved, the jury should not be entitled to find the necessary intention unless:

death or serious bodily harm was a virtual certainty from the defendant's actions, and the defendant appreciated that this was the case.

The murder conviction was therefore quashed and manslaughter substituted.

IN SIMPLE TERMS

What does the judgment mean?

There is a difference between thinking:

“This could cause serious harm.”

and:

“Serious harm is practically certain to happen.”

Woollin says the second situation can provide evidence from which a jury may find intention, even if causing that result was not the defendant's main aim.

THE LEGAL PRINCIPLE

Oblique intention and virtual certainty

Woollin is a leading authority on oblique intention.

Oblique intention deals with situations where a result was not the defendant's direct purpose.

If the result was virtually certain and the defendant appreciated that, a jury may find that they intended it.

Importantly, the jury is not automatically required to do so.

04

Why does this case matter?

The distinction matters because criminal law separates intention from simply taking a risk.

If a substantial risk were enough for murder, the law could treat people who foresaw danger in the same way as people who intended the result.

Woollin therefore sets a much higher threshold where the prosecution relies on foresight of consequences as evidence of intention.

THINKING ABOUT THE CASE

Was the decision convincing?

I think the higher threshold makes sense.

Murder is one of the most serious offences in criminal law. If the prosecution cannot prove a direct intention to kill or cause serious harm, something much stronger than simply recognising a risk should be required.

However, Woollin does not make intention completely mechanical.

Even where virtual certainty and the defendant's appreciation of it are established, the question of intention remains one for the jury.

That provides flexibility, but it also means the boundary between foresight and intention is not perfectly precise.

THE TAKEAWAY

If you remember one thing...

Woollin shows that intending a result and merely seeing a risk are not the same thing.

Where the result was not someone's direct aim, virtual certainty and the defendant's appreciation of that certainty can provide the basis from which a jury may find intention.
